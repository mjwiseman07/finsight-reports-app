# Schema Drift → ISA 315 Audit Assertion Mapping: Research Report

**Prepared for:** Advisacor — Phase MAJOR #2.3 ("Platform Integrity" card / `resolveAssertionImpact()` backfill)
**Research question:** Is there a defensible, audit-literature-grounded mapping from database schema drift signatures to ISA 315 / AS 2110 / SAS 145 audit assertions?
**Date:** August 5, 2026

---

## 1. Executive Summary

- **The mapping is defensible only at a conceptual, indirect level — not as a precise, literature-sourced one-to-one crosswalk.** Every authoritative standard reviewed (ISA 315 (Revised 2019), PCAOB AS 2110, SAS 145/AU-C 315) confirms that general IT controls (GITCs), which include database-layer controls, exist to protect the **integrity of information** — defined consistently across all three frameworks as **completeness, accuracy, and validity** — but all three standards also state explicitly that a general IT control (which is what schema-drift detection is) is "**typically not sufficient to address a risk of material misstatement at the assertion level**" on its own ([ISA 315 (Revised 2019), para. A150](https://www.ibr-ire.be/docs/default-source/nl/documents/regelgeving-en-publicaties/rechtsleer/normen-en-aanbevelingen/isa-s/isa-english-version/isa-315-revised-2019_en.pdf?sfvrsn=5d10e4d9_1)).
- **No first-party Big 4, PCAOB, AICPA, or IAASB document was found that maps specific database schema-drift signatures (missing column, renamed column, type change, FK violation, unique-constraint violation, NOT NULL violation) to specific named ISA 315 assertion categories.** The closest authoritative analogue is KPMG's ICFR Handbook, which discusses "data integrity risk" at the database layer and explicitly ties it to broad ICFR risk (not to the 8 named assertion categories) ([KPMG, *Internal Control over Financial Reporting Handbook*, 2023, Q6.4.110, Q7.2.x](https://kpmg.com/kpmg-us/content/dam/kpmg/frv/pdf/2023/handbook-internal-controls-over-financial-reporting.pdf)).
- **The strongest indirect authority is the completeness/accuracy/validity triad embedded in the ISA 315, SAS 145, and COBIT-2019/ISACA SOX definitions of "integrity of information" and "Managed Data" control objectives** — these terms overlap substantially, but not perfectly, with the ISA 315 assertion vocabulary (which additionally includes cutoff, existence, rights & obligations, classification, and presentation & disclosure). Treating "completeness/accuracy/validity" as fully interchangeable with the 8 ISA 315 assertions is an unsupported extrapolation.
- **Academic literature is empirical, not normative:** peer-reviewed accounting information systems research (Li, Peters, Richardson & Watson 2012, MISQ; Haislip, Peters & Richardson 2016, IJAIS; Mojtahedi & Zhou 2024, IJAIS) studies statistical associations between IT material weaknesses and outcomes like earnings quality, forecast accuracy, and CFO turnover. **None of the papers found establish a validated signature-to-assertion crosswalk** for schema-level drift events; they operate at the "IT material weakness category" level (e.g., "data processing integrity"), which is coarser than schema-drift signatures.
- **Recommendation: Ship with substantial modifications, not as-is.** Reframe `resolveAssertionImpact()`'s output from "these are the ISA 315 assertions violated" to "these are the assertions **for which the auditor should assess relevant risk**, contingent on materiality and downstream usage of the affected data" — mirroring how ISA 315 and KPMG's own handbook frame IT risk (indirect, contingent, requiring auditor judgment) rather than the founder's implicit framing of direct, deterministic assertion impact. Full detail in Section 8.

## 2. Framework Analysis: What ISA 315, AS 2110, and SAS 145 Actually Say

### 2.1 ISA 315 (Revised 2019)

**Definitions (Para. 12):**
- **Assertions** (12(a)): "Representations, explicit or otherwise, with respect to the recognition, measurement, presentation and disclosure of information in the financial statements... used by the auditor to consider the different types of potential misstatements." ([IAASB / IBR-IRE, ISA 315 (Revised 2019) full text](https://www.ibr-ire.be/docs/default-source/nl/documents/regelgeving-en-publicaties/rechtsleer/normen-en-aanbevelingen/isa-s/isa-english-version/isa-315-revised-2019_en.pdf?sfvrsn=5d10e4d9_1))
- **General IT controls** (12(d)): "Controls over the entity's IT processes that support the continued proper operation of the IT environment, including the continued effective functioning of information processing controls and the **integrity of information (i.e., the completeness, accuracy and validity of information)** in the entity's information system." (same source)
- **Risks arising from the use of IT** (12(i)): "Susceptibility of information processing controls to ineffective design or operation, or risks to the integrity of information (i.e., the completeness, accuracy and validity of transactions and other information)... due to ineffective design or operation of controls in the entity's IT processes." (same source)
- **IT application** (12(g)(i)): explicitly includes "data warehouses and report writers" — i.e., a Postgres-backed SaaS application and its schema squarely fall within scope.

**Key application-level statements:**
- Para. A150: "A general IT control alone is **typically not sufficient** to address a risk of material misstatement at the assertion level." This is the single most important sentence for Advisacor's use case — it directly undercuts any claim that a schema-drift signal *by itself* proves an assertion is compromised.
- Appendix 5, Para. 15: "The greater the complexity and volume of data... the less likely it may become for the entity to maintain integrity of that information through information processing controls alone... When the entity relies on general IT controls to maintain the integrity of certain information used by IT applications, the auditor may determine that the IT applications... are subject to risks arising from the use of IT." — Materiality and reliance are explicit gating conditions, not schema-drift facts alone.
- Appendix 5, Para. 18 (examples of risks arising from use of IT) — closely mirrors schema drift concerns: "Unauthorized access to data that may result in destruction of data or improper changes to data, including the recording of unauthorized or non-existent transactions, or inaccurate recording of transactions... Unauthorized changes to data in master files... Potential loss of data or inability to access data as required." Note: these examples describe **causes of risk**, not assertion-level conclusions — ISA 315 leaves the assertion determination to auditor judgment applied to the *specific account and disclosure* affected.
- Para. A190(a)-(b): the canonical 6 transaction-level assertions (occurrence, completeness, accuracy, cutoff, classification, presentation) and 6 balance-level assertions (existence, rights and obligations, completeness, accuracy/valuation/allocation, classification, presentation), which combine into Advisacor's 8-item catalog. Full text confirmed at same source.

**Conclusion:** ISA 315 gives Advisacor the *vocabulary* (integrity = completeness + accuracy + validity) and the *general risk categories* IT drift can cause, but does **not** provide a deterministic function from a technical signature to a named assertion. The standard is explicit that this determination requires professional judgment about the specific account/disclosure, materiality, and reliance — the opposite of a fixed lookup table.

### 2.2 PCAOB AS 2110 (Identifying and Assessing Risks of Material Misstatement)

Fetched directly from PCAOB ([AS 2110 full text](https://pcaobus.org/oversight/standards/auditing-standards/details/AS2110)):

- Para. .29: "The auditor also should obtain an understanding of how IT affects the company's flow of transactions... The identification of risks and controls within IT is not a separate evaluation. Instead, it is an integral part of the approach used to identify significant accounts and disclosures and their relevant assertions."
- Appendix B, Para. .B4 lists IT-specific risks almost identical to ISA 315 Appendix 5 Para. 18: reliance on systems inaccurately processing data or processing inaccurate data; unauthorized access resulting in destruction/improper changes to data, recording of unauthorized/non-existent transactions, or inaccurate recording; unauthorized changes to master-file data; potential loss of data or inability to access data.
- The standard does **not** use the term "application controls" explicitly (confirmed absent from the fetched text) and does **not** provide a signature-to-assertion table. Like ISA 315, it treats IT risk identification as embedded in, not separate from, the auditor's overall relevant-assertion analysis at Para. .59–.62.
- AS 2110 Para. .60–.61 lists the *risk factors* relevant to identifying "relevant assertions" generically (size/composition of account, susceptibility to misstatement, volume/complexity, etc.) — these are the actual drivers of assertion relevance, and none of them are IT-signature-specific.

**Conclusion:** AS 2110 is materially consistent with ISA 315: IT risk assessment is instrumental to, not a substitute for, the auditor's independent identification of relevant assertions per significant account.

### 2.3 SAS 145 (AU-C 315, effective for periods ending on/after Dec 15, 2023)

Confirmed via AICPA and Journal of Accountancy summaries ([AICPA SAS 145](https://www.aicpa-cima.com/resources/download/aicpa-statement-on-auditing-standards-no-145), [Journal of Accountancy, "Considering IT risk during audit risk assessment procedures," Nov. 2023](https://www.journalofaccountancy.com/issues/2023/nov/considering-it-risk-during-audit-risk-assessment-procedures/)):

- SAS 145 formally imports the ISA 315 (Revised 2019) definitions of "general IT controls," "IT environment," and "risks arising from the use of IT" verbatim, including the completeness/accuracy/validity definition of integrity.
- Per the Journal of Accountancy summary: "A general IT control alone is typically not sufficient to address a risk of material misstatement at the assertion level" — repeating the ISA 315 language.
- SAS 145 introduces characteristics that make an IT application *more* or *less* likely to carry risks arising from use of IT (interfaced systems, high transaction volume, complex functionality vs. stand-alone, low volume, simple functionality, hard-copy-supported transactions) — these are the actual determinants of whether a drift event matters, and they are entity/context-specific, not signature-specific.
- Draffin & Tucker's 2025 SAS 145/Cybersecurity training materials list the standard general IT control categories (access — authentication, authorization, provisioning, deprovisioning; change management; and computer operations — program changes, backup/recovery) ([Draffin & Tucker, SAS 145 and Cybersecurity, 2025](https://draffin-tucker.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/TFS-SAS145-and-Cybersecurity-DraffinTucker-2025.pdf)) — schema drift most naturally maps to the "change management" and "computer operations" GITC categories, not directly to an assertion.

**Conclusion:** SAS 145 does not diverge from ISA 315/AS 2110 in substance on this question. All three converge on: (1) database/schema-level controls are GITCs; (2) GITCs protect "integrity of information" (completeness, accuracy, validity); (3) GITCs alone don't determine assertion-level risk — that requires linking to the *specific account, disclosure, and process* the drifted data feeds.

## 3. Big 4 and Practitioner Precedent

**No public Big 4 methodology document was found that publishes a signature-level (schema drift → assertion) crosswalk.** What was found:

- **KPMG, *Internal Control over Financial Reporting Handbook* (2023)** ([full PDF](https://kpmg.com/kpmg-us/content/dam/kpmg/frv/pdf/2023/handbook-internal-controls-over-financial-reporting.pdf)) is the most detailed public document located. Key content:
  - Defines "Data integrity risk" (Q6.4.110): "Data integrity risks arise when information is incomplete or inaccurate because of how it is maintained within systems... Data integrity is generally addressed through GITCs over systems used to generate information." This is explicitly a *risk category feeding into a process risk point (PRP)*, not an assertion per se.
  - Defines the "Database" IT layer (Section 7.2): "Organizes data so it can be accessed, managed and updated. Examples: SQL Server, Oracle Database, stand-alone data repositories and data warehouses" — directly analogous to Advisacor's Postgres backend.
  - States a PRP (a candidate misstatement point) is *not* relevant to ICFR unless "it links to a relevant assertion" (Q4.3, para. "If a PRP does not link to a relevant assertion, it is likely not a relevant PRP for ICFR") — reinforcing that the linkage from technical event to assertion must be established at the level of the **specific process and account**, not generically at the level of "schema drift."
  - RAFIT (Risk Arising From use of IT) definition explicitly frames IT risk as "Susceptibility of automated controls to ineffective design or operation, **or** risk to IT-information integrity due to ineffective GITCs" — a disjunctive framing that keeps assertion-level linkage indirect.
  - Deficiency evaluation guidance (Section 9) states: "Application and database deficiencies are more likely to directly affect controls than operating-system deficiencies" — this is the *only* explicit statement found anywhere in the research suggesting the database layer is "closer" to assertion-relevant risk than other IT layers, supporting Advisacor's general intuition, but stopping short of naming specific assertions.

- **PwC** (Korean member firm ITGC guidance, and PwC UK "Restoring trust through risk management and internal control") discusses ITGCs as supporting confidence in automated application controls but does not publish an assertion crosswalk ([PwC Samil, ITGC guidance, 2023](https://www.pwc.com/kr/ko/insights/sgc/samilpwc_ksox-series_aug2023.pdf); [PwC UK](https://www.pwc.co.uk/audit/assets/pdf/restoring-trust-through-risk-management-and-internal-control.pdf)).

- **EY**, in its 2024 commentary on revised ISA 315 and IT risk, discusses the practical workload implications of the new ITGC/RAFIT requirements but does **not** discuss assertion-level mapping at all ([EY, "Revised ISA 315 and IT risks," June 2024](https://www.ey.com/en_lu/insights/assurance/revised-isa-315-and-it-risks-how-to-reduce-the-additional-workl)).

- **Deloitte** thought leadership located discusses data analytics as an audit *tool* (testing 100% of transactions) rather than IT-control-to-assertion mapping ([Deloitte, "Technology and the Audit of Today and Tomorrow"](https://dart.deloitte.com/USDART/ov-resource/c4db8900-39a8-11e7-a0c7-7588328ad2e8.html)). No Deloitte document mapping schema-level events to assertions was found.

- **A widely circulated audit-training deck** (unattributed to a specific firm but reflecting standard Big-4-style training content) states explicitly: "Database — controls typically address risks related to **unauthorised updates to financial reporting information** (e.g. through direct database access or execution of a program)" and reiterates, in capital letters for emphasis, "**A GENERAL IT CONTROL ALONE IS TYPICALLY NOT SUFFICIENT TO ADDRESS A RISK OF MATERIAL MISSTATEMENT AT THE ASSERTION LEVEL**" ([training material, "Lecture 9," hosted on Scribd](https://www.scribd.com/presentation/906972952/Lecture-9-8)). This is not a first-party authoritative citation (it is training content, provenance uncertain) and is included here only as corroborating color, not as a standalone citation-worthy source — flagged accordingly.

**Conclusion:** No authoritative source found for a Big 4-published, signature-level crosswalk. The uniform message across every accessible Big 4/practitioner document is that database-layer GITCs are relevant to *data integrity broadly* and can indirectly implicate assertions **through the specific account/process they support**, but the determination is context-dependent and requires auditor judgment — not a static table.

## 4. Academic / Peer-Reviewed Literature

No paper was found that builds or validates a signature-to-assertion crosswalk at the granularity Advisacor needs (missing column, renamed column, type change, FK violation, unique-constraint violation, NULL-constraint violation). The available literature operates one or two levels of abstraction higher — at the "IT material weakness (ITMW) category" level, tested empirically against financial-reporting *outcomes* (not assertions).

These are software-engineering-level constructs; the accounting literature engages with IT risk at the "material weakness category" level, not the database-schema-primitive level. This gap should be stated plainly to stakeholders.

## 5. COSO / COBIT Crosswalk

- **COSO 2013 Internal Control–Integrated Framework, Principle 11** ("The organization selects and develops general control activities over technology to support the achievement of objectives") is the COSO anchor point cited in KPMG's handbook. COSO Principle 11 requires GITCs to support "proper deployment," "continued proper operation," and "integrity of data and information" — but COSO's own points of focus for Principle 11 are written generically across all five components and do not name the 8 ISA 315 assertions (KPMG handbook, Section 5.2, citing COSO Principle 11).
- **ISACA/COBIT 2019 — "IT Control Objectives for Sarbanes-Oxley" (4th ed.)**: This is the single most concrete crosswalk-style document located. Under the **"Managed Data" control objective** (COBIT refs: APO01, APO14, DSS01, DSS04, DSS05), ISACA states: "Controls provide reasonable assurance that data recorded, processed and reported remain **complete, accurate and valid**... Data management includes the controls and procedures used to support information integrity, including **completeness, accuracy, authorization and existence**" ([ISACA COBIT 2019 SOX guidance, excerpted](https://www.studocu.com/fr-ca/document/hec-montreal/systemes-dinformation-en-gestion/it-control-objectives-for-sarbanes-oxley-97-118/121457358)). This is the **only authoritative document found that explicitly names "existence" alongside completeness/accuracy/validity** as a data-quality dimension tied to financial reporting controls — meaningfully closer to ISA 315 assertion vocabulary than ISA 315/SAS 145 themselves. However, it still (a) does not reference database constraints, referential integrity, schema-level controls, or any of Advisacor's six drift signatures by name, and (b) does not use the term "financial statement assertions," instead using "financial reporting" generically.
- The original 2003 ISACA/ITGI COBIT-COSO mapping document (submitted to PCAOB rulemaking docket 2003) maps COBIT's "Deliver and Support" domain process "Manage data" to COSO's "Control Activities" component generically, again without assertion-level granularity ([ISACA/ITGI 2003 filing to PCAOB](https://pcaobus.org/Rulemaking/Docket008/148_Information_Systems_Audit_and_Control_Association_(ISACA)_and_IT_Governance_Institute_(ITGI).pdf)).
- **GAIT methodology (IIA, 2007–08)**: retired IIA methodology for scoping ITGC testing under SOX 404. Confirmed via IIA's own page that GAIT does not provide assertion-level mapping and has since been retired ([The IIA, "The GAIT Series"](https://www.theiia.org/en/content/the-gait-series/)) — included here to close off a plausible-sounding but ultimately empty avenue.

**Conclusion:** COBIT/ISACA's "Managed Data" objective is the best-available crosswalk anchor because it explicitly lists **existence** alongside completeness/accuracy/validity — narrowing (but not closing) the gap to ISA 315's 8-assertion vocabulary. COSO itself does not add assertion-level granularity beyond what ISA 315/SAS 145 already provide.

## 6. Regulatory Guidance (SOX 404, PCAOB, SEC, AICPA)

- PCAOB's predecessor standard to AS 2110 — **Auditing Standard No. 12** (pre-reorganization; content now folded into AS 2110 Appendix B) — contains the identical IT-risk language found in AS 2110 today, confirming continuity of PCAOB's position across the AS/5 → AS 12 → AS 2110 transitions ([PCAOB, Auditing Standard 12 Appendix B](https://pcaobus.org/oversight/standards/archived-standards/pre-reorganized-auditing-standards-interpretations/details/Auditing_Standard_12_Appendix_B)).
- A 2007 SEC comment-letter response on PCAOB Release 2007-02 states: "To evaluate the significance of a deficiency in IT general controls, the effect of the deficiency on **application controls** should be evaluated" ([SEC comment letter, PCAOB-2007-02](https://www.sec.gov/comments/pcaob-2007-02/pcaob200702-38.pdf)) — this reinforces the "indirect, through application controls" chain that all frameworks describe: **GITC deficiency → application control reliability → assertion-level risk**, a three-hop chain, not a direct GITC → assertion link.
- No PCAOB, SEC, or AICPA document was found that states database schema drift, specifically, degrades any named assertion. This is consistent with the standards' overall posture: **they regulate the auditor's process for determining assertion relevance, not a deterministic taxonomy of IT-failure-to-assertion mappings.**
- **SAS 145's "at a glance" AICPA summary** confirms the standard requires auditors to "evaluate the design of certain controls within the control activities component, including general information technology (IT) controls, and determine whether such controls have been implemented" ([AICPA, SAS 145 At a Glance](https://assets.ctfassets.net/rb9cdnjh59cm/4xdHUrrmHTyuQq4oTamEHz/52db7960517384c69a9af3f2fcea88e3/sas-145-at-a-glance.pdf)) — again, an auditor evaluation process, not a lookup table.

## 7. Signature-by-Signature Mapping Table

For each drift signature, the table below states what the **literature actually supports** — distinguishing between (a) assertions with defensible textual grounding, (b) assertions plausible by extension of the completeness/accuracy/validity/existence vocabulary but requiring judgment, and (c) assertions with **no support found**.

| Drift signature | Assertions with defensible grounding | Assertions plausible only with added judgment/context | Assertions with no support found | Primary citation basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Missing column that code writes to (data loss)** | **Completeness** — directly matches ISA 315's completeness definition ("all transactions/events/balances that should have been recorded have been recorded") and the "integrity of information = completeness, accuracy, validity" definition in ISA 315 12(d)/(i) and SAS 145. | Valuation & Allocation (if the missing data feeds a calculated balance); Presentation & Disclosure (if a required disclosure element is silently dropped) | Cutoff; Rights & Obligations | [ISA 315 (Revised 2019) 12(d), 12(i), A190](https://www.ibr-ire.be/docs/default-source/nl/documents/regelgeving-en-publicaties/rechtsleer/normen-en-aanbevelingen/isa-s/isa-english-version/isa-315-revised-2019_en.pdf?sfvrsn=5d10e4d9_1); [KPMG ICFR Handbook, Q6.4.110 "Data integrity risk... information is incomplete"](https://kpmg.com/kpmg-us/content/dam/kpmg/frv/pdf/2023/handbook-internal-controls-over-financial-reporting.pdf) |
| **Renamed column (code silently reads NULL where data exists)** | **Completeness** (data effectively "disappears" from processing, same as a missing column from the application's perspective) and **Accuracy** (the recorded value — NULL — does not reflect the true underlying data) | Valuation & Allocation; Existence (if NULL causes a record to be excluded from an existence-dependent calculation) | Cutoff; Classification; Rights & Obligations; Presentation & Disclosure | [ISA 315 A190(a)(iii) "Accuracy—amounts and other data... have been recorded appropriately"](same as above); ISA 315 Appendix 5 §18 "unauthorized changes to data in master files" (analogous silent-corruption pattern) |
| **Type change (data truncation or coercion)** | **Accuracy** — squarely matches "amounts and other data relating to recorded transactions... have been recorded appropriately" (ISA 315 A190(a)(iii)) | Valuation & Allocation (truncation of a numeric/monetary field directly changes a recorded amount) | Completeness; Existence; Cutoff; Rights & Obligations; Classification; Presentation & Disclosure | [ISA 315 A190(a)(iii), A190(b)(iv)](same source); [KPMG Handbook Q6.4.50 "Integrity risks: data does not accurately transfer between systems"](same as above) |
| **Foreign key violation (orphaned records)** | **Existence** (an orphaned child record may reference a parent that does not exist — analogous to "assets/liabilities exist" failing at the relational level) — **weak/indirect support only; no source names FK violations explicitly** | Completeness (if the orphaning causes downstream aggregation to silently drop records); Classification (if the orphaned record can no longer be tied to its proper account/entity) | Accuracy; Cutoff; Rights & Obligations; Valuation & Allocation; Presentation & Disclosure | No direct authoritative source found for FK violations specifically. Extrapolated from ISA 315's general "risks to integrity of information" framing (12(i)) and KPMG's database-layer "unauthorised updates to financial reporting information" framing — **this row is the weakest in the table and should be flagged as judgment-based, not literature-grounded.** |
| **Unique constraint violation (duplicate records)** | **Completeness** and **Occurrence/Existence** are both plausible: duplicates can cause double-counting (an occurrence/existence problem — recording something that, in substance, did not happen twice) or, if de-duplication logic then discards records incorrectly, a completeness problem. | Accuracy (aggregate totals become wrong) | Cutoff; Rights & Obligations; Classification; Presentation & Disclosure | No source explicitly addresses "unique constraint violation." Extrapolated from ISA 315 A190(a)(i) Occurrence: "transactions... that have been recorded... have occurred" — duplicated transactions are the direct textbook example of an occurrence/existence failure in classic audit-assertion teaching materials ([ACCA, "The audit of assertions"](https://www.accaglobal.com/gb/en/student/exam-support-resources/fundamentals-exams-study-resources/f8/technical-articles/assertions.html) — note: ACCA is a professional-body educational source, not a standard itself, and does not discuss database constraints). |
| **NULL constraint violation (missing required data)** | **Completeness** — this is the most directly supported mapping in the table: an omitted required data element is definitionally a completeness failure under ISA 315 A190(a)(ii)/(b)(iii). | Accuracy (if the null propagates into a calculation as zero or a default, distorting a recorded amount); Presentation & Disclosure (if a required disclosure field is null) | Existence; Cutoff; Rights & Obligations; Classification | [ISA 315 A190(a)(ii), (b)(iii) Completeness](same source) |

**Overall pattern:** Across every signature, **Completeness** and **Accuracy** are the only two assertions with reasonably direct textual grounding in ISA 315's own definitional language (because ISA 315 defines "integrity of information" as completeness + accuracy + validity, and "validity" in ISA 315's own usage functions similarly to occurrence/existence). **Cutoff, Rights & Obligations, Classification, and Presentation & Disclosure have essentially no direct support** in any source reviewed for schema-drift-type events — these assertions are typically driven by *business logic and period-end processes*, not by database structural integrity, and no authoritative source connects them to schema drift. Any `resolveAssertionImpact()` output that claims cutoff, rights & obligations, classification, or presentation impact from a raw schema-drift signature is not literature-supported and should require additional context (e.g., which account/process the drifted table feeds) before being asserted.

## 8. Counterexamples and Caveats

The literature is consistent that schema drift does **not** automatically imply assertion risk. Specific conditions under which the mapping breaks down, all grounded in the sources above:

1. **Materiality gate.** ISA 315 Appendix 5 §15 and KPMG's ICFR Handbook (Q3.4.20) both make clear that risk assessment — including IT risk — flows from whether the affected **account or disclosure is significant**, which is a materiality judgment made independent of any IT event. A drift event in a table that feeds an immaterial account (e.g., a UI preference table, an internal analytics cache) creates no assertion risk regardless of signature type.
2. **Non-financial-reporting data.** ISA 315 12(g)(i) scopes "IT application" to systems used "in the initiation, processing, recording and reporting of transactions or information" that are relevant to financial reporting. Appendix 5 §19 explicitly notes that "an entity's IT environment may also include IT applications and related data that address operational or compliance needs" unrelated to financial reporting, and that such risks "may not necessarily affect financial reporting." A drift event on a table with no path into financial-statement-relevant data (e.g., a feature-flag table, a marketing-preferences table) creates no assertion risk under this standard.
3. **Compensating controls / redundant information controls.** KPMG's handbook (Q6.4.80) explicitly allows that "a different control activity can address completeness and accuracy of the same information" — meaning even a genuine data-integrity event may not translate to unaddressed assertion risk if a separate manual or automated reconciliation control catches the same misstatement. Advisacor's function cannot know, from the schema-drift signal alone, whether such compensating controls exist downstream.
4. **GITC-alone insufficiency.** As emphasized repeatedly (ISA 315 A150, SAS 145 per Journal of Accountancy summary, and the audit-training material cited in Section 3), a general IT control finding alone is **"typically not sufficient to address a risk of material misstatement at the assertion level."** This applies symmetrically: the *absence* of an ITGC finding (i.e., drift not yet detected) doesn't mean no risk exists, and a drift *event* doesn't mean assertion risk is proven — it means further evaluation (materiality, downstream use, compensating controls) is required.
5. **Detected-and-remediated drift.** If the scanner-authored drift event was detected and remediated before any financial data was actually written incorrectly (e.g., schema drift caught in a staging environment, or caught before the affected code path executed), no misstatement — and therefore no assertion impact — ever occurred. The mapping function should distinguish between "drift detected" (a control-operation event) and "drift caused an actual data integrity failure" (a misstatement-risk event) — current framing in the prompt's context does not appear to make this distinction.
6. **No authoritative source found for a "safe harbor" list of drift types that categorically never affect assertions** — this determination in every source is a facts-and-circumstances judgment tied to the specific account, not a categorical rule based on drift signature type.

## 9. Recommendation

**Do not ship `resolveAssertionImpact()` as-is, framed as an audit-literature-validated mapping.** Ship with the following specific modifications:

1. **Reframe the output semantics.** Change the function's contract from "this drift signature impacts assertions X, Y, Z" (a deterministic, evidentiary claim) to "this drift signature is a **risk indicator relevant to assertions X, Y** (per ISA 315/SAS 145's completeness-accuracy-validity definition of information integrity), **contingent on confirming (a) the affected table feeds a significant financial-statement account, and (b) no compensating control caught the issue.**" This mirrors how every authoritative source in this research frames IT risk — indirect and contingent, never direct and deterministic.
2. **Restrict claimed assertions to those with textual grounding.** Based on Section 7, only **Completeness** and **Accuracy** have defensible, citable grounding across all six signature types. **Valuation & Allocation** and **Existence/Occurrence** have grounding for a subset of signatures (type changes; unique-constraint violations, respectively) but require explicit reasoning, not blanket assignment. **Cutoff, Rights & Obligations, Classification, and Presentation & Disclosure should not be auto-populated by a schema-drift signature alone** — no source supports that link for any of the six signatures reviewed.
3. **Add a materiality/relevance gate before backfilling the 24 existing rows.** Per ISA 315 Appendix 5 §19 and KPMG Q3.4.20, before Phase 2.3 backfills assertion impact on the 24 scanner-authored rows, each row should be checked (even coarsely) against whether the affected table/column feeds a financial-statement-relevant account. Backfilling all 24 rows uniformly, without this filter, risks materially overstating assertion impact for drift events on non-financial tables — which is precisely the "counterexample" the audit literature warns against (Section 8, item 2).
4. **Do not present the "Platform Integrity" card to customers/auditors as ISA-315-validated methodology without a disclosed methodology note.** Given that no Big 4 firm, PCAOB, AICPA, or peer-reviewed source has published a schema-drift-to-assertion crosswalk at this granularity, presenting `resolveAssertionImpact()`'s output to auditors as if it reflects settled audit doctrine would overstate its evidentiary basis. A defensible middle path: label the card's assertion tags as **"risk indicators derived from IT general control monitoring, per ISA 315/SAS 145's definition of information integrity (completeness, accuracy, validity)"** with a footnote citing ISA 315 Para. 12(d)/(i) and disclosing that assertion relevance requires auditor confirmation against materiality and specific account impact — consistent with how the standards themselves require the auditor (not the software) to make the final determination.
5. **Do not claim the function was "audit-literature validated"** in patent, marketing, or compliance materials. The honest characterization, per this research, is: *grounded in the ISA 315/SAS 145/AS 2110 definitional framework for IT-related risk to information integrity, but not a literature-validated deterministic mapping — auditor judgment remains required to convert a flagged risk indicator into a conclusion about a specific relevant assertion.*
6. **Consider narrowing scope for v1:** given the strength of support specifically for Completeness and Accuracy, and near-total absence of support for Cutoff/Rights & Obligations/Classification/Presentation & Disclosure, a defensible v1 of the Platform Integrity card could report **only Completeness and Accuracy risk indicators** for schema-drift events, with the other assertions available in a "further review needed" state rather than auto-populated. This is more conservative but far more defensible if challenged by an external auditor or a customer's audit committee.
